# Cover Letter Writing Quality (no-ai-slop adaptation)

Scope: the cover letter body only. Never apply these rules to other application
answers.

Editorial rules adapted from Peter Yang's "no-ai-slop" skill
(https://github.com/petergyang/no-ai-slop, MIT), narrowed to a grounded cover
letter. The factual contract below outranks style whenever the two disagree.

## Factual contract (highest priority)

Editing for style never creates evidence. Use only what the master resume, the
job posting, and the company research already support. Do not add or change:
achievements, metrics or numbers, employer names, dates or titles, tools,
responsibilities, product or ownership claims, company or market facts, quotes,
examples, or opinions presented as facts. Do not widen scope ("supported" ->
"owned"), do not round or extend a number, and do not invent a company detail to
sound informed. If a sentence needs a fact you do not have, cut the sentence.

## Do one internal editorial pass before answering

Draft and edit inside this one response. Do not return the draft, do not add a
"What changed" section, do not explain what you changed, and do not mention
these rules in the output.

1. Draft the body from the grounded evidence.
2. Check the draft against the qualities and patterns below.
3. Remove only real slop, keeping every factual claim intact.
4. Return only the final body paragraphs, wrapped in the required tags.

## Edit for these qualities

- Lead with the point when the setup adds nothing.
- Prefer concrete facts and direct verbs to abstraction: "Owned the checkout
  funnel and ran weekly A/B tests" beats "drove significant optimisation".
- Keep the candidate's existing level of confidence. Do not inflate it, and do
  not hedge what the evidence already supports.
- Leave strong sentences alone. Do not rewrite a working sentence just to make
  every paragraph equally tidy.
- Keep the human cadence. Vary sentence length and shape; do not flatten the
  letter into one rhythm and do not stack punchy fragments for effect.
- Fix slop without compressing the substance out of the letter.
- Apply the portability test. If a sentence could be pasted into another
  candidate's letter or another company's application unchanged, it is filler:
  make it specific to this role, this company, or this evidence, or delete it.
- Use active voice with a human subject where practical.
- Keep the structure unless the structure is hurting the letter.

## Voice and content contract (how a senior PM writes to a senior operator)

Sound like an experienced operator writing plainly to another one: specific,
calm, evidence-first, confident without self-congratulation, commercially
literate, technically fluent where it matters. No performance of strategic
insight, no attempt to sound profound, no consultant-speak, no LinkedIn-post
cadence, no enthusiasm filler, no fake intimacy.

- Do not diagnose the company. Unless the job posting or company evidence
  states the problem, do not claim the company's growth problem, real challenge,
  or bottleneck, and do not speculate about its users' behaviour. Give a
  concrete reason the role is relevant instead.
- Do not manufacture a conceptual frame and position the candidate inside it
  ("that gap", "exactly that surface", "the traffic-to-conversion path", "has a
  specific shape", "the one thing that matters is"). Describe what the
  candidate actually did.
- Prefer the concrete mechanism to the metaphor. "Users dropped at the payment
  step, so I changed the payment ordering and reduced abandonment" beats "the
  checkout leaked on payment method mismatch".
- Do not interpret the job description for the reader. Do not label the role
  ("the growth-PM framing") and then announce that the candidate recognises the
  label. Name the actual responsibility, connect it to experience, and say why
  it is interesting.
- Do not diminish the company to elevate the candidate. No "messier than",
  "harder than", "more complex than", "more sophisticated than", or "I have
  already solved a tougher version of this".
- Do not end on generic company praise ("the European leader in the category").
  Use a company claim only when the supplied evidence states it and it adds to
  the candidate's motivation.
- Do not explain the company's own marketplace or business model back to the
  hiring team, and do not restate obvious company facts to prove that research
  was done. Use company context only when it directly supports the candidate's
  motivation or relevance, and prefer a direct close over a mini
  business-analysis paragraph.

## Evidence-selection discipline (two-story budget)

A cover letter is a selection, not a coverage report: it does not need every
supported fact, and leaving supported evidence unused is correct. Do not treat
unused evidence as a coverage gap. Prefer fewer, stronger, directly relevant
examples over maximum coverage.

- One employer story per evidence paragraph. Paragraph 1 uses one employer and
  one primary initiative; paragraph 2 uses one different employer and one
  primary initiative. A story is not just an employer name: it is one coherent
  responsibility or problem, then the mechanism or action, then the outcome. If
  the paragraph already has that chain, stop.
- At most two employers as candidate evidence in the whole body. No third
  employer in a side sentence, no third project hidden inside a paragraph, and
  no extra project in the close. Leaving relevant evidence unused is expected.
- Do not append a second initiative from the same employer after the main
  evidence already proves the point, and do not add side projects, secondary
  launches, adjacent initiatives, or "also..." achievements to strengthen a
  paragraph.
- Two-story budget: use at most two primary candidate evidence stories in a
  normal one-page letter, and make each one prove a distinct capability relevant
  to this role. Prefer the two strongest and most directly relevant stories.
- Do not insert an achievement because it exists in the resume; insert it
  because it answers "why is this useful for this specific role?". If that
  answer is weak, leave the example out.
- Story-selection test, applied to every candidate story: is it one of the two
  strongest for this role; does it prove something materially different from the
  other selected story; is it more relevant than the evidence already selected;
  and would the letter be weaker if it were removed? If the answer to the last
  question is no, omit the story.

Metric discipline: metrics support a story, they are not an inventory.

- No more than two outcome metrics per primary story. Outcome metrics include
  percentages, percentage points, revenue figures, time reductions, parity
  percentages, conversion or abandonment lifts, and scale figures used as proof
  of impact.
- Context counts such as number of markets, rollout duration, team size, or
  country count may be used sparingly for context, but must not be stacked with
  multiple outcome metrics until the paragraph reads as a metric inventory.
- Choose the best two outcome metrics for each story, not every supported
  metric. Do not add a weaker metric after stronger metrics already establish
  the point, and do not add a metric from a tangential domain to raise evidence
  density.
- Avoid stacking several percentages in adjacent sentences unless each one is
  essential to the role fit.

Silent selection before drafting: pick the best two stories for the role, pick
 the best two outcome metrics for each story, discard every weaker or tangential
piece of evidence, then draft only from that selected set. Do not expose this
selection or reasoning in the output.

## Structure contract (content logic, not fixed wording)

- Paragraph 1: why this role is relevant, plus one employer story. Reference one
  or two real responsibilities from the posting, connect them to the candidate's
  experience, then give one primary initiative as responsibility, concrete
  mechanism, and one or two key outcomes. A simple opening is fine. Do not
  diagnose the company, invent user behaviour, or open with a broad marketplace
  theory. Do not add a second employer, a second initiative from the same
  employer, or a side achievement.
- Paragraph 2: the second distinct story, from one different employer. Give one
  primary initiative as responsibility, concrete mechanism, and one or two key
  outcomes. No "lesson" sentence after the evidence; if the result already
  proves the point, stop. Do not return to the first employer for a mini-story,
  do not add a third employer or a side project, and do not list every
  achievement.
- Paragraph 3: direct motivation and close. Name the actual part of the role the
  candidate wants to own and how the two stories already given transfer to it,
  then stop. It may add practical logistics if directly relevant (for example
  location or hybrid availability). Do not introduce new evidence: no new
  candidate evidence, new metrics, a third employer or project, a new
  achievement, prestige praise, or company or user-segment analysis, and do not
  explain the company's own business model. Prefer a short close.

## Hard bans (acceptance-blocking)

These are not suggestions. A deterministic checker enforces the high-confidence
forms below, and a body that trips it is corrected once before it can be
accepted. When the intended meaning is valid, state the positive claim directly.

- Binary contrast templates. Do not write "not X. It is Y", "not X. It's Y",
  "not X, but Y", "not just X, but Y", "not because X, but because Y",
  "less about X, more about Y", "The lesson was not X. It was Y", "The point is
  not X. It is Y", or "The question is not X. It is Y". Do not use "rather than"
  as a rhetorical contrast ("a retention lever rather than a notification
  pipe"). Ordinary grammatical negation that carries real meaning is fine; the
  banned forms are the ones that set up a punchline instead of stating it.
- Interpretive metadiscourse. Do not write "The lesson is", "The takeaway is",
  "The key point is", "What this shows is", "This is the part", "The X mindset
  is the part I recognise", "Both experiences point to", "I have spent my career
  on exactly that surface", or "This is exactly the kind of problem". The
  evidence should carry the point; delete the commentary.
- Unsupported company diagnosis. Do not open with an invented diagnosis such as
  "the company's growth problem is", "the company does not have a demand
  problem", or "the company's real challenge is" unless the job posting or
  company evidence states it. Never convert destination names, inventory size,
  or company research into a speculative claim about the company's actual
  bottleneck.
- Sloganized abstractions. Do not write "X, Y and Z are one funnel", "the
  acquisition and conversion layers have to be built as one system", "the
  traffic-to-conversion surface", "the product decisions that move them", or
  "where intent decays", and do not lean on surface, layer, engine, lever,
  flywheel, or motion as abstract product metaphors. Name the concrete action,
  metric, or mechanism instead.
- Colon reveals. Do not use dramatic colon-reveal constructions. Colons are fine
  for real lists, labels, and quotes.

## Patterns to remove

- Throat-clearing openers ("Here's the thing", "Let me be clear").
- Faux-insight setups ("What most people miss", "The part everyone skips").
- Generic contrarian claims that state nothing checkable.
- Binary "not X, but Y" contrasts that add no information.
- Negative listing ("Not a X. Not a Y. A Z.") and dramatic fragments.
- Colon reveals ("The best part: it learns").
- Superficial analysis clauses ("highlighting", "underscoring", "reflecting").
- Importance puffery ("marks a pivotal moment", "a testament to").
- Weasel attribution ("experts agree", "studies show", "reports suggest").
- Interpretive metadiscourse: commentary that tells the reader what the
  preceding evidence proved. "The lesson that transfers is...", "Both
  experiences point at the same operating model...", "This shows...", "What
  matters here is...". Delete the commentary and let the evidence carry it.
- Restating what the previous paragraph already proved.
- Generic self-praise ("I do my best work on", "I am passionate about").
- Fake-profound endings and mic-drop final lines.
- Summary or recap endings that restate the letter. End on the last concrete
  point.
- Robotic symmetry: identical paragraph shapes, repeated sentence openers.
- Generic corporate vocabulary where a concrete phrase exists.

## Words to cut

Banned unless quoted or part of a proper name: delve, foster, leverage, utilize,
facilitate, empower, streamline, robust, cutting-edge, paradigm shift, game
changer, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, paramount,
transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness, ever-evolving.

Often empty: just, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly, fundamentally,
importantly, crucially, inherently, inevitably; and phrases such as "it's worth
noting", "at the end of the day", "when it comes to", "at its core", "in terms
of", "with regard to", "in order to".

Em dashes: the JQuest letter ban stays. Use none.

This is not a banned-word filter. Context decides: a listed word that carries the
candidate's real voice or a real distinction stays.

## Self-check before answering

1. Does every sentence pass the portability test, or is it specific to this
   role, this company, or this evidence?
2. Is any factual claim new, changed, or inflated? If so, cut or restore it.
3. Does any sentence trip one of the hard bans above, especially a binary
   contrast or a metadiscourse marker? If so, rewrite it to state the positive
   claim and run the self-check again.
4. Does the letter avoid diagnosing the company, manufacturing a
   "gap/surface/shape" frame, interpreting the role for the reader, and
   comparing the company unfavourably to past work?
5. Does each evidence paragraph use exactly one employer and one primary
   initiative, with no "also..." side achievement and no third employer or
   project anywhere in the letter?
6. Are the metrics limited to at most two outcome metrics per story, with no
   weaker or tangential metric added after stronger ones already prove the
   point, and no context counts stacked into a metric inventory?
7. Does the close stay short, avoid new evidence and new metrics, and avoid
   explaining the company's own business back to it?
8. Is the ending concrete rather than profound or a recap?
9. Read aloud, does it sound like a person making a case rather than polished
   machine prose?

Then return only the final body paragraphs in the required tags.
